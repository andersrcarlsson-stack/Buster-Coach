The opponent and its strength

Your opponent is a chess engine: Buster, which comes with the app, or any other UCI engine you choose under Engine → Engines…. The slider in its strip sets its strength.

What the levels mean. Levels 1 to 10 are meant to play like human players of rising strength, and level 11 is the engine at full strength.

Level Plays roughly like
1 · Beginner a lichess rapid player of about 1000
2 · Novice about 1150
3 · Casual about 1300
4 · Improving about 1450
5 · Club player about 1600
6 · Strong club about 1750
7 · Tournament player about 1900
8 · Strong tournament about 2050
9 · Club champion about 2200
10 · Top amateur about 2400
11 · Full strength the engine at full strength — far beyond human

The numbers are lichess rapid ratings, not FIDE ratings: online ratings run higher, so a lichess 2200 is roughly a FIDE 1900–2000 club player. The ratings belong to the built-in Buster: the levels were measured with it. With another engine the levels keep their order, but not their ratings, so the slider then shows the names only.

Each level is meant to be a clear step up from the one below: if you beat a level about two games out of three, the next one is about right for you.

How a level plays. A real player doesn't lose because they think too little; they lose by mistakes: a move that looked fine to them, punished by something they didn't see. How far a player sees is what their rating is about. The levels copy that:

  • the engine thinks less at the lower levels, which gives it the small inaccuracies;
  • and each level has a horizon: how far ahead it looks when it judges a move. Now and then it plays the move that looks best within its horizon instead of the engine's own choice. Usually that's a fine move; but when the position hides a trap just beyond the horizon, it walks into it, as a player of that rating would. A beginner may leave a piece where it can simply be taken; a 2200 player never does, but can miss a combination three moves deep.
  • like people, it plays carefully just after the opening, and makes most of its mistakes in the middlegame. When it errs, the saved game marks the move with "?" and says what it should have played, and at Guided and Full coaching a card tells you "Buster slipped. There's something here." Spotting and punishing mistakes is a big part of getting better.

It never blunders in a position that is already decided: a won ending is played out properly.

How the levels were made. The ratings are estimates, not promises. We measured how often lichess players of each rating make mistakes, and how big those mistakes are — about 3000 games for every rating band, analysed by a strong engine — so that each level's mistakes look like theirs. Then the levels played each other, and Stockfish at a known strength, close to 2000 games in all, and were tuned until they won about as often as their ratings say. Levels 4 to 10 are now close to their ratings; levels 1 to 3 still play stronger than their labels, more like 1400–1500. What we couldn't do is play them against real people at those ratings. If a level feels too strong or too weak for your rating, we'd like to hear about it.

Full strength thinks up to a minute on a hard move in an untimed game (about ten seconds on average); "Play now" makes it move at once. In timed games it uses its clock.

The opening. At the start the opponent plays moves from an opening book, as players do: how many moves deep depends on the level.
